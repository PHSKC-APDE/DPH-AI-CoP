Background
The aardvark (Orycteropus afer), the sole extant species within the order Tubulidentata, has intrigued zoologists and paleontologists for centuries. Major contributors to our understanding include early anatomists, ecological physiologists, and modern-day conservation biologists.

Georges Cuvier initially classified the species in the genus Orycteropus, emphasizing its distinctive cranial morphology [8].
Richard Lawson Skinner and John D. Skinner provided detailed reviews of the aardvark’s anatomical and physiological adaptations in the Nama Karoo, focusing on digging behavior and water balance [5].
Lucas J. Legendre and Jennifer Botha‑Brink conducted the first detailed histological study on limb bone microarchitecture, linking it to fossorial activity [3].
Benjamin Rey et al. investigated drought impacts on physiology and survival in the Kalahari, highlighting vulnerabilities to climate change [2].
In captive settings, Jennifer Wojick, Karen Terio, and others analyzed male reproductive anatomy via imaging and histology [21].
Jennifer Hamilton, Grace Fuller, and Stephanie Allard assessed enrichment effects on zoo‐housed aardvarks, advancing knowledge of behavioral welfare [1].
These researchers and institutions—ranging from the San Diego Zoo Wildlife Alliance to the University of the Witwatersrand—have been instrumental in shaping contemporary understanding of aardvark biology, behavior, and conservation.

Discussion
Taxonomy and Evolution
Aardvarks are the only living members of the order Tubulidentata, family Orycteropodidae, and genus Orycteropus [8, 10]. They are afrotherians, sharing an evolutionary lineage with elephants, hyraxes, golden moles, and tenrecs, with origins tracing back to the Paleocene (~65–70 Ma) [8, 10]. Fossil relatives emerged during the Eocene, while the genus Orycteropus appears in Miocene deposits (~18–19 Ma) [5, 10]. Their unique tubulidentate dentition—lacking enamel and composed of hexagonal dentinal tubules—is a defining feature, cited in early taxonomical works [10].

Morphology and Physiology
Adult aardvarks typically weigh 40–65 kg and measure up to 2.2 m in length, including the tail [23]. They possess a robust, muscular build; large forelimbs with spade-like claws; elongated snouts; large, sensitive ears; and coarse sparse fur [23]. Distinctive “tube teeth” lack enamel, and cheek teeth exhibit continuous growth [21]. Internally, male reproductive structures include primely internal testes at the inguinal canal, accessory glands (seminal vesicles, prostate, bulbourethral), and prominent preputial scent glands—analyzed via ultrasonography and computed tomography [21].

A 2025 histological review highlighted several specializations:

Well-developed salivary glands and muscular stomach aid prey ingestion.
Nocturnal behavior and thermally buffered burrows help avoid diurnal heat.
Limited water requirement, met through dietary intake [5].
Limb Bone Histology and Fossorial Adaptations
Legendre and Botha‑Brink (2018) studied limb bone histology (humerus, radius, ulna, femur, tibia, fibula) in adult and subadult aardvarks. They found:

A predominance of compacted coarse cancellous bone (CCCB) in midshaft cortices, formed via compaction of trabeculae originating from metaphyses.
Minimal periosteal bone, with a thin outer cortical shell—suggesting cortical drift.
CCD patterns that contrast with other large mammals and reflect a functional compromise influenced by fossorial demands, energy limitations, and semi-arid habitats [3].
Significant biomechanical constraints on bone microstructure due to ground excavation combined with physiological strains from myrmecophagy [3].
These findings reinforce the importance of considering both structural and energetic determinants when inferring fossorial lifestyles from bone histology.

Sensory Biology
Vision and nocturnal sensory adaptations have been explored. Peichl et al. (2025) analyzed retinal architecture and photoreceptor distributions:

Rod densities: 124,000–214,000 rods/mm² (central to peripheral retina), moderate relative to other nocturnal mammals.
Small yet present cone population (300–1,600/mm²), accounting for 0.25–0.9% of photoreceptors.
Both L and S cone opsins exhibited co-localization, suggesting limited color discrimination.
Presence of a reflective tapetum lucidum of choroidal tapetum fibrosum configuration enhances nocturnal vision [20].
Retinal avascularity contributes to pronounced film thickness and rod density limitations [20].
These adaptations reflect a visual system finely tuned to low-light environments.

Behavior and Enrichment
Wild aardvarks are strictly nocturnal, solitary, and forage primarily for ants/termites using acute olfactory and auditory senses. They excavate extensive burrow systems (2–13 m long, up to 6 m deep) and may cover 6–19 miles nightly to locate insect colonies [23, 27].

In captivity, Hamilton et al. (2020) investigated enrichment strategies for zoo‑housed aardvarks:

Enrichment aimed at promoting foraging or exploration influenced activity and social interactions differently.
Foraging enrichment elicited immediate response; exploratory enrichment was more effective later at night.
Increased affiliative behaviors and decreased agonistic interactions were observed, supporting positive welfare impacts tied to goal‑oriented enrichment [1].
Habitat and Geographic Distribution
Aardvarks are widely distributed across sub-Saharan Africa, from South Africa to Ethiopia and Sudan, but absent from dense forests and hyper-arid deserts like the Sahara. They adapt to savanna, grassland, woodland, and semi-arid habitats with sandy or loamy soils favorable for burrowing [23, 28].

Their distribution closely aligns with the presence of termites and ants. They avoid very rocky or compacted soils where digging is laborious. They also occur at elevations up to 3,200 m (e.g., Bale Mountains) [9, 27].

Ecosystem Function and Climate Vulnerability
As ecosystem engineers, aardvark burrowing significantly alters abiotic and biotic components of soil:

Soil redistribution through mounds and excavated burrow entrances enhances heterogeneity in moisture, compaction, and temperature.
These modifications promote plant species richness and composition, influencing community assemblages [6].
Rey et al. (2017) implanted biologgers in adult aardvarks during a severe Kalahari drought:

Five of six study individuals died. These deceased animals exhibited progressive drops in nocturnal body temperatures (nadir as low as 25 °C), indicative of starvation and heterothermia.
Activity shifted from nocturnal to diurnal, but homeothermy broke down, indicating negative energy balance and collapse of thermoregulation [2].
Soil moisture deficits during drought reduce insect prey availability, leading to nutritional stress even if temperatures remain within normal ranges [2].
These findings emphasize how climate change can indirectly threaten aardvark populations via trophic disruption, with cascading effects on ecosystem stability.

Conservation and Research Gaps
Although classified as “Least Concern” by IUCN, aardvark populations are impacted by climate variability, habitat destruction, and limited zoo representation (36 individuals across 18 US institutions) [21].

Remaining research gaps include:

Comprehensive demographic monitoring across multiple populations to assess drought recovery and reproduction.
Long-term studies on climate-driven shifts in termite/ant abundance and correlating aardvark fitness.
Expanded histological analysis across age groups and geographic regions to refine fossorial signaling.
Genetic studies to evaluate population structure and resilience across declining ranges.
Given their ecological role, conservation efforts focused on habitat integrity, termite availability, and climatic mitigation are essential.

Conclusion
Aardvarks represent a unique lineage among mammals—ecologically important, anatomically specialized, and evolutionarily distinct. Key points include:

Taxonomic uniqueness: Today’s lone representative of Tubulidentata and Orycteropodidae, integrated into Afrotheria.
Structural adaptations for subterranean insectivory: robust limbs, long claws, tubular dentition.
Bone microstructure signatures: compacted cancellous bone predominates over periosteal bone, reflecting fossorial stress [3].
Visual and sensory specialization: moderate rod density with reflective tapetum; co-localized cone opsins hint at low color perception [20].
Anthropogenic and climatic pressures: burrow engineering fosters biodiversity; drought-related starvation underscores vulnerability to environmental stress [2].
Zoo-based welfare insights: enrichment aimed at specific behaviors enhances well-being [1].
Future efforts should integrate physiological monitoring, demographic surveys, habitat management, and climate modeling to safeguard aardvark populations and the ecological niches they support.
